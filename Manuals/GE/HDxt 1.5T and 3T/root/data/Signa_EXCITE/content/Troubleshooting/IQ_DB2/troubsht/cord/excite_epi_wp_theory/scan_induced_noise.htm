White Pixel - Theory

Ambient Screen Room Noise?

You may recall that spike noise is just an electrical discharge occurring in, or being sensed in, the RF screen room.  More often than not, these arcs and sparks are as a result of all of the high-powered, electrical things that we do to make an MRI image.  High power RF circuits, Pin diode biasing currents, High current gradient drives, and the large amounts of vibration generated due to the rapidly changing gradient fields in a large static magnetic field...  all can be the source of our noise spikes.  But before we go off half-cocked, trying to figure out which part of our complicated system is the culprit, wouldn't it be nice to know that we are starting off with a nice quiet, noise free scan room environment to begin with?

One trick to accomplishing that is by making use of the spike noise detection hardware built into the system:  Yep, that's right, the good ol' TNS!!  Here's how:

Hook up the Head coil and either leave it at the home position, advance it to isocenter, or advance it out the rear of the magnet.  It doesn't really matter because you will not be scanning.  However, it will be a bit more sensitive to room noise if the head coil is outside the magnet bore due to the inherent RF shielding of the Body coil.  Now, close up the scan room tight.  Ensure that the UTNS is not emulated out in the mgd_stage file (or that the TNF is in the "Enabled" position at the rear of the Systems Cabinet), and then remove the small white cable (or cables) going into the BNC connections at the bottom of the TNF assembly.  They will be labeled "Data in Window" and/or "Receiver Unblank".

Remembering from the last episode, these two signals only gate the counter.  When you disconnect these cables you will enable the counter full time.  Both the Head and Body coil preamps will already be getting their 15-volt bias from the TNF itself.  (Note: the only exception to this is in the case of the new Excite systems.  On Excite systems, the Body preamplifier still gets its 15-volt bias from the TNS, but the Head Preamp which is built into the new "Integrated Head T/R Assembly", gets it's 10-volt operating bias from a regulator on the Multi-coil assembly, which is in turn fed by a 15-volt supply in the Driver Module.)

Now any spikes that happen in the scan room, even without running a scan, will register as an event on the counter!!  How cool is that?

Zero the counter, then leave it monitoring for a while...  Go get a cup of coffee...  get some dinner...  go call home and tell your "significant other" that you're going to be working late tonight...  then check the counter when you get back.  You should see no increment.  Zero.  Nada.

If you are detecting spikes (without even scanning) don't bother doing other types of spike noise troubleshooting until you resolve that first.  You can try turning power off to nearly everything in the scan room:  DC lights, power to electrical outlets, blowers, patient monitoring equipment, etc...  even including the various subsystems of the MRI system itself: Cold Head, Gradient and Patient cooling fans, SRI, PAC, Magnet enclosure displays, etc.  Just remember that you will need to maintain power to the preamp and (obviously) the TNS itself, so that part of the System Cabinet will have to stay powered on.

 

Scan Induced Noise

OK, so you've done the ambient noise check above and your screen room appears to be noise free, at least when you are not scanning.  But the customer is still seeing lot's of corduroy?  Must be some spike noise that is being induced only when scanning.  Oh my...  What to do next?  

Well, you could try to reproduce the problem by running the exact same scan series the customer is running and looking at the raw data by modifying the cv "rawmode" to a 1.  Or maybe by saving the raw data and viewing it with the MRImage tool.  It might come to that eventually, but that is sure to be some very slow going.  Your best bet is to run the EPI White Pixel (epiwp) tool.  This tool is fully documented on the various Service Methods CDROMs, so I will not go into how to set it up and run it, except to say:  

You'll want to run your initial and final epiwp testing in mode 1 (all axes, all bandwidths).  This is your Go/No-Go survey.
Once you see some spiking in any of the mode 1 images, switch over to running epiwp in mode 2, which is where you will be looping on the worst-case image from the mode 1 survey and viewing the images.

In all likelihood, if you have any kind of scan induced white pixel problem, "epiwp" is going to shake that bad-boy out.  In fact, you are quite likely to find some spike noise modes that your customer hasn't even found yet.  "epiwp" is pretty much the weapon of choice in the big battle.

The EPI White Pixel Flowchart  goes into several methods that you can use to isolate the spike noise source(s).  I suggest that anyone going to do "Spike Noise Battle" reference it, especially prior to your first encounter.

A couple of things to keep in mind regarding the EPI white pixel tests: 
When you are installing a system or upgrade, you will be required to run the "White Pixel" subtest of EPT prior to performing EPT full calibration.  You will also need a report that the system has no EPI white pixels in order to generate an "Install in Spec" key.  It has been noted that this subtest may calculate the threshold slightly differently than "epiwp" mode 1 which will effect reporting of spike counts.  The "epiwp" run from a shell should be used only for troubleshooting.  EPT White Pixel subtest should be used for installation validation.  In both cases the TNS should be disabled during spike noise testing, but should be enabled during EPT calibration.
