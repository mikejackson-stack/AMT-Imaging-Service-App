1.5T Excite White Pixel Theory

 

Transient Noise Filter Theory

Considering how common spike noise is, and how easy it is to inadvertently create it, even in the controlled environment of an RF screen room; GE design engineers searched for a means to make the Signa MRI system less sensitive and reduce the vulnerability to their inevitable occurrence.  A few different schemes were considered, but eventually it was decided that their approach would be a hardware solution that would detect and "blank out" spikes before they even reached the MR receiver.  The name of this device was originally the Transient Noise Suppressor (TNS) but that was later changed to Transient Noise Filter (TNF).  It is actually the exact same device. 

 

New Excite 2A (11.0 M3a) and later systems have a completely redesigned TNF, called the Universal TNS (UTNS3).  Since this is not yet fully functional, we can't really say much about it yet, except that the concept is very promising in that the UTNS will have a separate antenna in the scan room and it's own "receiver" just for detecting broadband events.  For the remainder of this discussion we will be referring to the old "tried and true" TNF that we have come to know and love, which is currently installed in the vast majority of the installed base.

 

Below is a simplified block diagram of a single TNF channel.

Physically, the TNFs are located in the Systems cabinet.  Functionally, they are located between the preamplifiers and receivers in each receive channel.  There will normally be one TNF Assembly with multiple TNF channels, one for each receive channel.  Multi-coil systems will have a 3-channel TNF expansion module, which piggybacks to the original 2-channel TNF assembly.

 

First, the input signal is split.  Part of it goes through a notch filter, which is centered on the nominal magnet frequency.  This filter will reject signals a set bandwidth above and below the nominal magnet frequency, but pass any other received signals.  This is what prevents the TNF from detecting and blanking the desired NMR signal.

 

An Enable / Disable switch is provided in the TNF Assembly to control the detection and blanking of all channels.  When enabled, the filtered signal goes to the Spike Detection circuit of the TNF channel.  Any signal that gets past the Notch Filter, since we have established that it is not an NMR signal, must be broadband noise and will therefore be detected.  Spikes thusly detected will trigger actuation of the Transient Blanker Switch.  When the blanker switch is actuated it interrupts the normal signal path and places a nominal impedance on the receive line.  This is so the receive signal does not drop to zero when blanked, which would itself cause problems in the reconstructed images, but will instead go to some nominal noise value for the duration.

 

The Spike Detector also sends a signal to the TNF event counter.  There is only one counter for all TNF channels in the assembly.  The counter will only increment once for any simultaneously detected spike on the multiple TNF channels.  However, keep in mind that each TNF channel is capable of detecting and blanking independently.  The event counter will also only increment if the "Data in Window" and "Receiver Unblank" signals are both true (high).  Note that these control signals have the effect of only gating the counter.  A detected spike event will still actuate the Transient Blanker Switch regardless of the state of the "Data in Window" and "Receiver Unblank" signals.

 

The split input signal path from the preamplifiers that does not go thru the notch filter is the normal receive signal path.  This signal is amplified in a Gain Stage to compensate for the insertion losses of the rest of the circuitry.  The net overall gain of the circuit should still be 1:1.

 

By the time the Spike Detector is able to sense a noise impulse and the Transient Blanker Switch is actuated, the spike would likely already be halfway to the receiver.  To combat this, a delay function is added to the normal signal path.  This aligns the blanking relative to the spike noise for complete suppression.

 

A 15-volt preamplifier bias is output on the RF input cable of each channel, at all times.  There is also a DC block on the RF Output circuit of each channel to prevent loading down the preamplifier bias supply circuit of the transceiver.

 

TNF Troubleshooting

There are many things that can go wrong with the TNF that will cause problems with the imaging system.  Since there are many active components in the design, it is entirely possible for the TNS to negatively effect the receive signal even when there is no spike noise present, or the switch is placed in the "Disable" position.  Either low gain or a noisy TNF channel can negatively impact image quality through reduced SNR.  A channel with excessive gain has been seen to be the cause "Over-ranged Receiver" images.

 

The first logical step in troubleshooting a suspected bad TNS is to bypass the questionable channel (or channels) by connecting the TNS Input cable to the TNS Output cable using a BNC bullet connector.

 

If the symptoms change, you will know that you are on the right track.  However, this does not necessarily prove that the TNF is faulty.  You need to be aware that it is possible for the TNF to sense an "out of band" signal, which it would interpret to be broadband noise, but which is actually some other discrete (correlated) noise that happens in that frequency range.  You should use the SPT correlated noise test (with the TNS bypassed) to be sure.

 

You should also be aware that excessive spike noise in the scan room can, and will, cause ghosting in images due to an excess of the desired NMR signal being blanked.  This may be mistaken as a faulty TNS when, in reality, the TNS is functioning exactly as designed to.  The simple test to rule this out is to place the TNS in the "Disable" position and scan.  If the problem is due to excessive blanking the images will go from being ghosted to having corduroy or other image noise problems, at which point you could begin chasing white pixels.

 

Of course, a TNF could also malfunction such that it no longer blanks when it should, when spike noise does occur.  This would be evident as corduroy artifact in images even when the TNF is in circuit and enabled.  The TNF assembly has a built-in spike generator, and a Functional test procedure has been developed and documented on the MR Service Methods CD ROM to functionally test each TNF channel.

 

 

TNF Philosophy

 

The overall design goal of the TNF was to eliminate any intermittently occurring spike transients, but to be otherwise invisible to the data acquisition.  By eliminating the "once in a while" spikes that inevitable occur in any scan room the MR operator will not become sensitized to their occurrence.  However, if excessive numbers of spikes occur, the TNF will continue to blank them along with meaningful NMR data, which would negatively impact the final image.

 

This was why a counter circuit was included in the design.  The idea was that if too many events were counted we would know that it was time to go in and troubleshoot a severe spike noise problem.  The counter was even intended to be remotely accessible via a serial connection so that proactive notification of an impending white pixel problem could occur.  Unfortunately, the TNF counter has always been unreliable as an indicator, primarily because the gating signals we use do not accurately describe the actual sampling time of the receiver.  Additionally, the software hooks to support remotely querying the TNF counter were never fully developed.

 

Through the years there have been differing opinions as to the utility and application of the TNF.  Many Field Engineers have adopted the stance that the TNF is only a "band-aid" and should only be enabled after a customer has already complained of corduroy artifacts, which as we know is caused by spike noise.  Isn't that a bit like closing the barn door after the cow has already got out?

 

On the other hand, by leaving the TNF enabled and active at all times as it was designed, any intermittent occurrences of spike noise will be eliminated, masking them from the operators.  This could help to improve their perception of the system and preclude them from overly scrutinizing the IQ.  The hazard here is that we may not become aware of an impending problem until it becomes so severe that the images become blurry and ghosted due to the excessive blanking.
